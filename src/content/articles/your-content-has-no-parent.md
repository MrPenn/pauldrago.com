## Two to three months, then two weeks

A new marketing executive came in and found out that <data value="c:postcard">a holiday postcard</data>, end to end, was going to take two to three months. Her jaw dropped, and it should have. This was a product we had been promoting for years, on a four by six card, in our standard design system. Nobody was revolutionizing the postcard. So she sat in every meeting. Brief, copy, design, legal, list pull, proofs. If a review was due Thursday she was in the reviewer's office Thursday, and it went out in two weeks.

<figure class="cd-img"><picture><source srcset="/assets/postcard-dark.webp" type="image/webp" media="(prefers-color-scheme: dark)"><img src="/assets/postcard.webp" width="1200" height="720" loading="lazy" alt="A postcard buried under approval stamps; one corner of the picture still shows."></picture></figure>

Same people, same tools, same postcard. Nothing was bought and nobody was hired. It was a Herculean effort, and what it exposed was everything in this piece: a product that had not changed in years still went through the whole chain from a blank page every time, and no stage in that chain measured how long work waited in front of it.

She could not sit in every meeting for every postcard, and she did not have to. With what the first one exposed, we componentized everything on that card that could be componentized, and for the next two to three years the incremental postcard reviews stayed under two weeks without anyone senior in the room. She kept track by hand once. The system kept track after that.

## Most stages are never measured

<p class="cd-deck">The wait is the same in every industry, because the chain is the same, and the surveys say so.</p>

I ran the content operation inside marketing at a bank holding company, and before that at a global industrial manufacturer. The bank had regulators reading every disclosure. The manufacturer had a few dozen markets rebuilding the same sell sheet. In both, a routine asset took <data value="c:routine-wait">one to two months</data> from request to use: a landing page, an email, a branch flyer.

<p class="cd-stat"><data value="c:adobe-approval-stages">89%</data> of marketers say content goes through three or more approval stages. <data value="c:adobe-review-time">58%</data> spend more than 40% of their time on reviews and approvals. <data value="c:adobe-people">47%</data> say a single asset involves 51 to 200 people.</p>

*Adobe, survey of more than 1,600 marketers, June 2025. Self-reported time share; Adobe sells the remedy.*[^1]

Requests waited at intake because nobody was allowed to say no, so everything was accepted and nothing was prioritized. Reviews had no clock, and nobody ever got in trouble for sitting on one.

The product owner who has to approve the piece is <data value="c:plane">building the plane after jumping off the cliff</data>. It is the Wile E. Coyote moment. Reading a piece to work out what they don't like about it, what needs to change, and what has to be adjusted is real work, and when they prioritize their day it is never the top one or two things on the list. So it sits. Nobody is being lazy. The chain just asked a person with a plane to build to do a second job with no deadline attached.

<p class="cd-stat">57% of creative teams say an asset needs <data value="c:review-versions">3 to 5 versions</data> before it is done. 25% say <data value="c:review-versions">six or more</data>. <data value="c:ziflow-compliance">88%</data> report compliance issues traced to a poor review process.</p>

*Ziflow with the American Marketing Association, State of Creative Workflow, July 2023. Sample size not published; Ziflow sells proofing.*[^2]

Translation queued behind the last thing that was sent, and build queued behind everything above it. Six or seven review rounds was normal. Some assets never converged until someone senior said ship it.

The library told the same story from the other side. In the libraries I have audited, somewhere between <data value="c:audited-reuse">a fifth and two fifths</data> of what got made was ever used again. Regions rebuilt what already existed because finding the thing was harder than making it, and both versions went through review and translation. The phrase people used for how work moved between stages was "over the fence."

<figure class="cd-record" data-record="creativex-unactivated"><p class="cd-record-num"><data value="c:creativex-unactivated">52%</data></p><div class="cd-record-body"><p class="cd-record-lead">of core creative assets were never activated in any channel,</p><p class="cd-record-more">across 1,284 assets and 422,000 posts in 50+ markets.</p></div></figure>

*CreativeX, 2023 data, published February 2024. Large consumer brands; "activated" means seen in channels CreativeX monitors; CreativeX sells the measurement.*[^3]

You will also see "<data value="c:folklore-60-70">60 to 70 percent of B2B content goes unused</data>" in every deck on this subject. It comes from a 2013 conference remark with no method or sample ever published, so it is folklore with a date. The CreativeX figure is measured, and <data value="c:creativex-unactivated">it says half</data>.[^4]

## Content is data

<p class="cd-deck">Every asset is a record. The discipline for tracking records already exists, and marketing never borrowed it.</p>

An asset is a file. Nothing records what the file is made of, and nothing records where it went, so you can count the files and you cannot reconcile them. Every other part of the company has a name for that: a table with no IDs, no record of where anything came from, and no owner.

Data teams already track what a record is made of, where it went, who owns it, and which version was live on a given day.

Content is data. Every asset is a record with fields. Every approved component (a claim, a disclosure, a rate, a spec, an image with its rights cleared) is what data teams call <data value="c:master-data">master data</data>: the one approved version that every asset built from it should point back to. Once you say that out loud, the vocabulary for tracking and owning it is already in the building, in the data organization, with tooling and thirty years of practice. Marketing never borrowed it because marketing has always thought of its output as creative, and creative work does not get IDs.

The data organization is not a finished example. Gartner's July 2024 survey of 1,203 data management leaders found <data value="c:gartner-ai-ready">63 percent</data> do not have, or are unsure they have, the right data management practices for AI, and Gartner expects <data value="c:gartner-ai-ready">60 percent</data> of AI projects without AI-ready data to be abandoned through 2026.[^6] The discipline exists; it is unevenly applied, and marketing is about to walk into that gap with generative AI.

<data value="c:postcard">The postcard executive</data> was doing by hand what a data team does with a map of every record and where it came from. She could see every record and every dependency, for two weeks.

Borrow three of its words, and the conversation about content supply changes from opinions to records.

<data value="c:lineage">Lineage</data> is the first: the record of what each asset is made of and where each part is used. Run it downward and you see what a change touches: before you change a rate or expire a disclosure, you see the 400 assets in 12 languages that carry it. Upward, you have reuse, measured: the share of shipped assets with a parent, the number of components per asset that came from the library. Data teams already have open tools for it.[^7]

Master data is the approved component. Legal signs the disclosure block once and it is approved in every asset that uses it, and translation attaches the same way. That one move collapses six review rounds into one, and it is what makes AI safe to use, because a machine assembling from approved parts leaves only the connective copy for a human to read.

<data value="c:version-history">Version history</data> is the audit. A regulator does not ask what the disclosure says today. They pick a day in the past, <data value="c:march-14">say March 14</data>, and ask what the customer saw on it. A data warehouse answers that every day by keeping every version of a row with the dates it was current.[^8] A content system that keeps only the current published version cannot answer it, and most of them keep only the current published version.

> In a data warehouse, "show me every asset that carried this disclosure between March and June" is a query. In a shared drive it is a month.

Data engineers also <data value="c:pipeline">draw a pipeline as a chain of jobs</data>, each depending on the one before, with a timestamp on every handoff. The content chain fits it: brief, create, translate, review, build, publish. Drawn that way, queue time between jobs becomes a measurement.

## Pharma already runs it this way

<p class="cd-deck">Pharma attaches approval to the claim, and it measures what happens.</p>

Pharmaceutical promotion goes through medical, legal, and regulatory review, called MLR, on every piece. The industry's answer to that load, over the last decade, was to build claims libraries and modular content: each claim approved once with its references, each module a pre-approved block, each asset assembled from modules with the review reading the assembly. It is the model in this piece, running in a regulated industry, on a system of record that publishes its benchmarks.

High performers: slightly more than one.</p><p class="cd-record-more">Average review and approval time: <data value="c:veeva-approval-days">21 days</data>.</p></div></figure>

*Veeva Pulse Content Metrics, anonymized system data across 350+ life sciences companies, 2021 through 2023.*[^9]

Set that next to marketing: <data value="c:review-versions">4 versions per asset</data> on Filestage's platform data, <data value="c:review-versions">3 to 5</data> for most of Ziflow's respondents, <data value="c:review-versions">6 or more</data> for a quarter of them.[^2][^10] The claim was approved before the asset existed, so the review has less to read.

The before-and-after exists too, with the caveats a vendor release deserves. Two unnamed Veeva customers reported early results from modular content in 2023: one cut time to market by more than half, <data value="c:veeva-modular-results">raised reuse 40 percent</data>, and cut approval time 30 percent; the other got 75 percent of approvals through in a single cycle and cut costs 19 percent.[^11] Sanofi, writing about why it went all in, gave the industry breakdown: <data value="c:sanofi-breakdown">three weeks to market</data>, six days in draft and fifteen in MLR review.[^12]

And the thing pharma had to fix first is the thing this piece is about. In 2016, <data value="c:veeva-2016-traceability">81 percent</data> of life sciences companies surveyed could not report where their claims and content were in use, and 70 percent had no central global library.[^13] No lineage, no master data. A decade later they have the benchmarks above.

In Veeva's own CRM data, <data value="c:veeva-unused">77 percent</data> of approved field content was rarely or never used in 2022, and <data value="c:veeva-unused">nearly 80 percent</data> by 2025.[^14]

Even in the model system, with every claim approved and every module tracked, most of what gets made sits. Approval attached to the component fixes the review. Deciding what to make, and what to stop making, is a separate job, done by a person at intake.

## Content with no parent costs money and invites fines

### Dark content

<p class="cd-deck">Dark data with a cost attached. Assets with no parent, times cost per asset.</p>

<data value="c:dark-content">Gartner defines dark data</data> as the information an organization collects, processes, and stores in the course of business and generally fails to use for anything else.[^21] Splunk asked 1,300 managers in 2019 how much of their data was dark and the average answer was <data value="c:splunk-dark-data">55 percent</data>.[^22] Content has the same thing, and the reason it exists is the same: nothing recorded what the record was for or where it went.

<figure class="cd-img"><picture><source srcset="/assets/no-parent-dark.webp" type="image/webp" media="(prefers-color-scheme: dark)"><img src="/assets/no-parent.webp" width="1200" height="720" loading="lazy" alt="A stack of identical documents with luggage tags; every tag is blank except one."></picture></figure>

An asset built from a downloaded file has no parent. It went through review and translation and cost what any asset costs, and it is invisible to every lineage query. The share of shipped assets with no parent is your dark content number, and counting it is simple: count the assets that shipped, count the ones with a declared parent, and the gap is content the system cannot account for. Multiplied by cost per asset, it is the money spent outside the system every year.

Adobe's 2025 survey found <data value="c:adobe-asset-volume">70 percent</data> of organizations producing at least 1,000 assets a year and two fifths producing more than 10,000, so <data value="c:worked-assets">5,000</data> is a conservative figure for a mid-sized global shop.[^1] Lineage coverage at a shop that has never had the field is generous at <data value="c:worked-lineage">40 percent</data>, given that CreativeX measured <data value="c:creativex-unactivated">half of core assets never activated</data> and pharma, with a system of record, still logs <data value="c:veeva-unused">77 percent</data> rarely used. Fully loaded cost per asset, with agency fees, internal hours, translation, review time, and rework divided by what shipped, is a number finance has to build. No public benchmark for it exists; I looked. <data value="c:worked-cost">$1,800</data> is a round placeholder, and yours replaces it.

<p class="cd-stat"><data value="c:worked-assets">5,000</data> assets &times; <data value="c:worked-lineage">60%</data> with no parent &times; <data value="c:worked-cost">$1,800</data> = <data value="c:dark-content-cost">$5.4 million</data> a year outside the system.</p>

*Asset volume from Adobe's 2025 survey; lineage coverage a placeholder informed by CreativeX and Veeva; cost per asset a placeholder for your own figure.*[^1][^3][^14]

Some of that spend was necessary. Nobody can say which part, and that is before the duplication those assets caused, the translation of things that were already translated, and the compliance exposure of disclosures with no record of where they ran.

Lineage breaks when someone downloads an asset, changes it, and uploads it as new.

> Every upload declares a parent, or it does not publish. A new asset is fine, as long as it says it is new.

### The regulator's question

<p class="cd-deck">It is written into the rules, and one firm has been fined for not being able to answer it.</p>

Every bank that advertises a deposit account works under <data value="c:reg-dd">Regulation DD</data>, which asks it to keep copies of its advertisements for two years and to be able to reconstruct the required disclosures.[^16] A bank with a broker-dealer arm also answers to FINRA, and <data value="c:finra-2210">Rule 2210</data> spells out the whole record: every retail communication kept for three years, with a copy, the dates of first and last use, the name of the principal who approved it and the date they did, and the source of any statistical table or chart.[^15] That is the record, whichever rule binds: the asset, the dates it was live, who approved it, and the lineage of every figure in it.

| Rule | Binds | What it requires | Retention |
|---|---|---|---|
| <data value="c:finra-2210">FINRA 2210(b)(4)</data> | Broker-dealers | Copy, dates of first and last use, approver and date, source of any statistic | 3 years |
| <data value="c:sec-17a-4">SEC 17a-4</data> | Broker-dealers | Copies of all communications sent and their approvals; electronic records in WORM or with a full audit trail | 3 years |
| <data value="c:advisers-204-2">Advisers Act 204-2(a)(11)</data> | Registered investment advisers | A copy of each advertisement disseminated | 5 years from last use |
| <data value="c:reg-dd">Regulation DD 1030.9(c)</data> | Banks and savings associations; not credit unions | Evidence of compliance, including copies of advertisements; "institutions must be able to reconstruct the required disclosures" | 2 years |
| <data value="c:reg-z">Regulation Z 1026.25(a)</data> | Creditors, including banks and credit unions | Evidence of compliance, with advertising expressly exempt from the retention requirement | 2 years |
| <data value="c:fdic-328">FDIC Part 328</data> | Insured banks, and anyone describing deposit insurance | No false or misleading representation of deposit insurance; digital signage by April 1, 2027 | None stated |

Credit unions sit outside Regulation DD and follow the NCUA's version, Part 707. Regulation DD's commentary says sample copies of advertisements are enough, so FINRA's list is the stricter of the two, and a content record built to it covers both.[^16] Regulation Z exempts advertising from its retention rule, so a lender's ability to prove what an ad said rests on its own records and its UDAAP defense, with no mandate behind it.[^17] <data value="c:fdic-328">FDIC Part 328</data> gives insured banks until April 1, 2027 to put the FDIC's digital sign on their websites and apps. A sign that repeats across a site and an app is a component: approved once, placed everywhere, with the date it took effect.[^15]

## Reuse is the default

A request queue will accept the same brochure from four regions. What stops that is <data value="c:triage">a person at intake</data> with the library in front of them and the authority to close a request.

A region asks for a flyer for the new small-business checking account, in Spanish, for the chamber breakfast in six weeks. Triage finds two, the business banking one that shipped in March and the Houston branches' adaptation of it in May, links both, and closes the request as fulfilled. The region comes back to say the Houston one is close, but the photo is wrong for their branches and the event line needs to go. Triage tells them to reopen it with that, since a photo swap and a deleted line is <data value="c:variant-lane">the variant lane</data>: two days, and review reads only what changed. Then the region asks what happens if they need a new claim on it. Triage says that makes it a new piece, through the whole chain, compliance included. When triage asks which claim, the region says never mind the claim.

Sometimes the claim is real, and then it deserves the whole chain. Most of the time it is a region testing whether anyone will sign for it.

Give a person who is responsible for the outcome a choice between something new and something that exists, and they will pick new every time. We see it with every launch, every event, every product. They believe new gives them the best chance to stand out, and they are emotionally invested in the result, which they should be, because the P&L is theirs. The belief is still wrong. The people they are targeting have never seen the existing asset, or anything like it, so the perfect new version they are picturing is perfect for an audience of one: the requester.

<!-- slop-ok: unsourced-figure (an operator's idiom, not a measurement; Paul kept it, 2026-09-25) -->
Our job as their marketing partner is to tell them how long new really takes. New is six weeks, and it will get pushed, and it will get delayed. The one that is 90 percent right is in their hands by the end of the week. Put those two next to each other and most people take the asset. Ask them "do either of these work for you?" and they hear that new is on the table, and they take new.

The variant lane covers an image swap, a call to action, a market line, connective copy, nothing that touches an approved component. A production designer spins the variant from the parent, review reads the diff, and turnaround is a day or two. The new-piece lane covers anything that touches a regulated component or the layout, and it goes through the full chain.

### Jurisdiction, language, and voice

<p class="cd-deck">Where the law differs, the market gets its own record, with its own owner.</p>

I am a native English speaker with no real grasp of any other language, and I did not understand, for longer than I should admit, how different the Spanishes are from each other, or that a US marketing team has no business deciding what a product can claim in Mexico. Both of those cost us.

The one I think about went like this. The English got translated into Spanish. Fine. Clean translation, on time, into the template. <data value="c:mexico">It did not count under Mexican law.</data> The product could not make the same claims there that it could make in the US, so the piece was not ready for market, and every asset built on it had to be redone in a hurry because now there was a problem. The translation had done its job. The job was the wrong one.

In master data terms, a translation is the same record in another language. Global owns it, translation is a service, review is linguistic, and below the regulated components, machine translation with a human post-edit is fine. The throughput numbers for attaching translation to the component are measured.

<p class="cd-stat">A translator working from scratch: <data value="c:translation-throughput">about 1,100 words an hour</data>. On a 100% translation memory match: <data value="c:translation-throughput">about 2,460</data>. Post-editing neural machine translation in a banking domain: <data value="c:translation-throughput">up to 60% faster</data> than translation memory alone.</p>

*Parra Escart&iacute;n and Arcedillo, MT Summit 2015, ten professional translators; L&auml;ubli et al., MT Summit 2019, four translators, German to French and Italian. Small samples, single language pairs.*[^23]

A localization is a different record that fills the same slot. Mexico's disclosure says different things because Mexican law requires different things. It is its own record, owned by Mexican legal, with its own version history. Group legal at the parent company reads it for brand and enterprise risk. What it says under Mexican law is Mexican legal's, and it does not wait in the US queue for that.

There is a layer between the two that gets lost as well. Mexico and Spain are both Spanish-speaking audiences with their own linguistic voice, and a piece that reads as Castilian in Monterrey reads as somebody else's. CSA Research surveyed 8,709 consumers in 29 countries and found <data value="c:csa-own-language">76 percent</data> prefer to buy with information in their own language and 40 percent will never buy from a site in another language.[^24] Voice belongs to the market, in the post-edit, and it is cheap next to what the law layer costs.

The way most global shops actually run it: the Mexican disclosure goes to a translator, comes back word for word, and someone in Mexico rewrites it in a Word document that never gets back into the system. Next campaign, same thing, and the system has no record of the version that ran.

A US bank meets the same three layers without leaving the country. A Spanish-language savings ad in Houston has to make the same <data value="c:reg-dd">Regulation DD</data> disclosures as the English one, and US compliance owns them in both languages. Voice belongs to the branches that know the customers. And <data value="c:cfpb-lep">the CFPB's 2021 statement</data> on customers with limited English suggests a slot the English ad never needed: a notice, in Spanish, of "the extent and limits of any language services" the bank offers.[^38]

So the model needs slots, a place in the template that resolves to a market-owned record, and the schema needs jurisdiction and language as separate fields. For every component, ask whether a market is different because of language, because of voice, or because of law.

## Review reads the diff

<p class="cd-deck">Legal finds the hole in that rule in the first meeting, so close it before promising anyone speed.</p>

Two approved components next to each other can make a misleading whole, and a compliant disclosure under a new claim is what a regulator reads. So the template and its allowed combinations get pre-approved, assembly inside the template is covered, and only a novel combination triggers a full read. <data value="c:veeva-review-cycles">Pharma's 1.3 cycles</data> is what that looks like when it holds.

Triage flags novel. Legal decides, from a list of combinations short enough to read in an afternoon, which is a reason to model only the <data value="c:twenty-components">20 components</data> behind most of the volume and leave the rest as pages. And the assembly gets a name on it, because when a pre-approved combination turns out misleading in context, legal will ask whose.

Reading the diff also fixes the reviewer's problem from the postcard. <data value="c:plane">A product owner with a plane to build</data> will not put a full read at the top of the day. Two changed lines and a swapped image, they will do between meetings, which is most of why the variant lane moves at all.

> Triage is a political job. Every diagram draws it as a box, and it is a person who says no to a regional director once a day and gets escalated over once a week.

The escalation always sounds the same, and it comes in three kinds. Some people go find the most senior person they know who cares about the product or the outcome and get them to appeal the decision. Some try to lie about the work, or find another way to maneuver around triage. And some accept it to your face, then complain quietly and politic around it anyway. Those are what you get, and the executive who said reuse is the default has to be willing to hear all three.

Agencies lose billable rounds and notice, and the reopen reason turns into boilerplate. Triage holds only with an executive who has said, in front of the regions, that reuse is the default; with the reopen data published monthly; and with the agency contract rewritten around variants instead of rounds.

The reopen data cuts both ways, and it should. When a region keeps rejecting the same parent, that is a scorecard on the center, and <data value="c:creativex-unactivated">CreativeX's 52 percent</data> reads as easily as "global makes what local cannot use" as it does as waste. <data value="c:postcard">The postcard executive</data> was triage for two weeks. The job is making that permanent, and making the log something the regions can point at too.

### Buy generation last

The variant lane is where AI belongs: image and copy variants, assembled from approved parts inside a locked template. The tools that check generated work against brand and product guidelines are only as good as the guidelines they check against. If those guidelines are the approved components, the tools help. Buy them before the model exists and you get one-offs faster, each of which goes through full review and full translation, and the pile in front of legal gets taller.

<p class="cd-stat">In 2026, <data value="c:typeface-speed">34%</data> of marketing leaders needed one to two months to launch a campaign, up from 5% in 2025. <data value="c:typeface-speed">92%</data> needed ten or more stakeholders to sign off; 44% needed twenty or more, up from 10%. 88% say their teams generate content quickly and struggle with sign-off.</p>

*Typeface Signal Report, June 2026, 200+ marketing leaders at VP and above. Small sample; an AI content vendor; year-over-year across different respondent pools.*[^27]

The Content Marketing Institute's 2026 survey says the same thing from the other side: <data value="c:cmi-ai-use">95 percent</data> of B2B organizations use AI tools, 89 percent for content creation, and 39 percent report the content performing better.[^28] Gartner's 2026 CMO survey puts <data value="c:gartner-cmo-ai">15.3 percent</data> of marketing budgets into AI, with 30 percent of CMOs saying their organization is ready to scale it and 70 percent saying their internal processes lack the maturity.[^29] Adoption is near universal, the process did not change, and the campaigns got slower.
